Structure

Main Conclusion

 Identify the final conclusion that the rest of the argument is built to support.
"Which one of the following most accurately expresses the main conclusion of the argument?"		 Find the support chain. Ask 'Why?'. If another claim supports it, it's a conclusion. If it supports nothing else, it's the main conclusion. (The Why Test) The #1 Trap Intermediate Conclusions. A claim that is supported by evidence but also goes on to support the final conclusion.
Structure

Argument Part

 Describe the specific function a particular phrase or sentence serves in the argument.
"The claim that X plays which one of the following roles in the argument?"		 Ask: 'Does it support something?' and 'Is it supported by something?'. Focus on the structural function, not the subject matter. The #1 Trap Content vs. Function. Wrong answers often accurately describe the content but misidentify the role (e.g., calling evidence a conclusion).
Patterns & Flaws

Method of Reasoning

 Describe the logical technique or strategy the author uses to build their argument.
"The argument proceeds by"		 Abstract the argument into general terms. Identify the premises, conclusion, and how they relate. Match your abstraction to the answer choices. The #1 Trap Half-Right Answers. An answer might correctly describe one part of the argument but completely misrepresent another.
Patterns & Flaws

Parallel Reasoning

 Find the answer choice that uses the exact same logical structure as the stimulus.
"Which one of the following arguments is most similar in its reasoning to the argument above?"		 Match the conclusion type (absolute vs. conditional), premise structure, and validity. Strip away the topic. The #1 Trap Same Topic, Different Logic. Wrong answers often use the exact same subject matter but completely different logical relationships.

Patterns & Flaws

Flaw

 Identify the specific reasoning error committed in the argument.
"The reasoning in the argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that"		 Play Devil's Advocate: Ask 'How could the evidence be perfectly true while the conclusion is still completely wrong?' The #1 Trap Describing a Flaw Not Committed. The answer describes a real logical fallacy, but not the one that actually occurred in this argument.
Patterns & Flaws

Parallel Flaw

 Find the answer choice that commits the exact same logical error as the stimulus.
"The flawed pattern of reasoning in the argument is most similar to that in which one of the following?"		 Identify the specific flaw (e.g., conditional logic error, part-to-whole). Match only that specific structural error in the choices. The #1 Trap Matching Valid Logic. An answer choice that has the same conclusion type but is actually a valid argument.
Argument Impact & Assumptions

Evaluate

 Identify the question or piece of information that would be most helpful in determining if the argument is strong.
"Which one of the following would it be most helpful to know in order to evaluate the argument?"		 Use the Variance Test: Supply opposing extremes (e.g., Yes/No, 0%/100%). If one strengthens and the other weakens, it's the right answer. The #1 Trap Irrelevant Context. A question that might be interesting but whose answer doesn't change the validity of the conclusion.
Argument Impact & Assumptions

Strengthen

 Find the new piece of information that makes the conclusion more likely to be true.
"Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the argument?"		 Identify the conclusion and the evidence. Find the gap. Look for an answer that patches the gap or blocks a potential counterattack. The #1 Trap Justifying a Premise. An answer that strengthens a premise that is already accepted as true, rather than strengthening the link to the conclusion.

Argument Impact & Assumptions

Weaken

 Find the new piece of information that makes the conclusion less likely to be true.
"Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?"		 Identify the core argument. Find the gap or assumption. Attack that gap with new evidence that shows why the conclusion doesn't have to follow. The #1 Trap Attacking a Premise. You cannot argue with the premises on the LSAT; you can only show they don't necessitate the conclusion.
Argument Impact & Assumptions

Sufficient Assumption

 Find the missing premise that, if added, guarantees the conclusion is 100% true.
"The conclusion follows logically if which one of the following is assumed?"		 Look for Dangling Terms (a new concept in the conclusion). Find the Gap (Entry, Middle, End). Insert the answer and rerun the chain. The #1 Trap Necessary but not Sufficient. An assumption that must be true for the argument to work, but isn't enough on its own to prove the conclusion.
Argument Impact & Assumptions

Necessary Assumption

 Find a premise that the argument requires or depends upon to work.
"Which one of the following is an assumption required by the argument?"		 Use the Negation Test. Logically negate the answer choice. If the negated version destroys the argument, it is the correct necessary assumption. The #1 Trap Sufficient but not Necessary. An answer that would fix the argument perfectly, but goes too far (extreme language) and isn't strictly required.
Principles

Principle Strengthen

 Find a broad rule that, if applied, helps justify the specific reasoning in the stimulus.
"Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning?"		 Identify the specific leap made in the argument. Find a generalized rule that bridges that exact gap. The #1 Trap Rule is Too Narrow. A rule that applies to the specific topic but doesn't cover the logical jump made.

Type Task & Stem Method Trap
Principles

Principle Generalize

 Extract the underlying general rule from a specific scenario or argument.
"Which one of the following generalizations is most clearly illustrated by the situation described?"		 Summarize the lesson or moral of the story. Strip away the specific characters and items. The #1 Trap Overly Specific Rules. Answers that retain too many details from the original stimulus rather than generalizing.
Principles

Principle Apply

 Take a broad rule and apply it to a new specific situation to see which one fits.
"Which one of the following judgments most closely conforms to the principle stated above?"		 Convert the rule into a strict checklist (sufficient/necessary conditions). One missed necessary condition can eliminate an answer. The #1 Trap Reversed Logic. An answer that affirms the consequent or denies the antecedent of the given principle.
Inference

Most Strongly Supported

 Find the statement that is best proven by the combination of facts in the stimulus.
"Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?"		 Combine facts. Look for overlaps. The correct answer doesn't have to be 100% proven, but it should be very close based only on the text. The #1 Trap Real-World Truths. Statements that are true in the real world but not supported by the specific text provided.
Inference

Fill in the Blank

 Complete the logical thought at the end of the stimulus, usually a conclusion.
"Which one of the following most logically completes the argument?"		 Treat it like a Must Be True or Main Conclusion question depending on the preceding context. Synthesize what was just said. The #1 Trap New Directions. An answer that brings up a new, albeit related, point rather than concluding the current line of thought.

Inference

Must Be True

 Find the statement that is 100% guaranteed by the facts in the stimulus.
"If the statements above are true, which one of the following must also be true?"		 Ask: 'Could this possibly be false without contradicting the stimulus?'. Combine absolute statements and conditional chains. The #1 Trap Exaggeration. Words like 'all', 'never', 'must', when the stimulus only supported 'some', 'rarely', 'might'.
Inference

Must Be False

 Find the statement that completely contradicts the facts in the stimulus.
"If the statements above are true, which one of the following must be false?"		 Use the Coexistence Test. Can this statement and the stimulus both be true at the same time? If no, it's the answer. The #1 Trap Could Be False. Statements that might not be true, but aren't strictly proven false by the stimulus.
Paradox / Explain

Paradox / Explain

 Find the piece of information that resolves a seeming contradiction between two facts.
"Which one of the following, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy?"		 Identify both surprising facts. Preserve both facts as true. Find the hidden distinction, bridge, or alternate cause that allows both to coexist. The #1 Trap Explaining Only One Side. An answer that explains why one fact is true, but makes the other fact even more confusing.
Dialogue

Disagree

 Identify the exact point of contention between two speakers.
"The dialogue provides the most support for the claim that X and Y disagree over whether"		 Speaker-by-Speaker Commitment Test. Would Speaker A say 'Yes' or 'No'? Would Speaker B say the opposite? If one speaker has no opinion, it's wrong. The #1 Trap Agreeing to Disagree. An issue that both speakers mention, but actually agree on, even if they disagree on the broader conclusion.

Dialogue

Agree

 Identify a statement that both speakers are committed to believing.
"The dialogue provides the most support for the claim that X and Y agree that"		 Speaker-by-Speaker Commitment Test. Would Speaker A say 'Yes'? Would Speaker B also say 'Yes'? The #1 Trap Unstated Opinions. A statement that one speaker clearly agrees with, but the other speaker's stance is completely unknown.
